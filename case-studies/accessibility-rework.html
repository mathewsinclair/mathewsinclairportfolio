Backing it up

In 2025, 1,416 of 5,114 US digital accessibility lawsuits were against sites that already had a widget. Our own fixes reduce the risk. An untested widget can add to it.

Source: UsableNet 2025 year-end report. Raised as a risk, not a legal judgement.

The widget now sits in the background and only opens from accessibility links. I've asked the vendor to confirm it doesn't change the page until someone opens it.

Involving the website team early would have saved the business time and money.

Approach · Key decision

The typescale: accessible, but still on brand.

Small text was behind 59% of the remaining Pope Tech alerts, and the live site went as small as 9px. I pitched a 16px base. Leadership wanted to keep the brand's small type, so I settled on 14px.

Why 14px still works

WCAG doesn't set a minimum font size. It requires that text can be resized to 200% without losing content (1.4.4). So as well as raising every size, I moved the system to rem and em units, so text scales with browser zoom and people's own settings.

Results · The hurdle

Making accessibility part of the workflow.

The hardest part wasn't technical. Accessibility had been agreed as a priority, but there was no shared check in the design process. When the product page was redesigned outside the accessibility work, fixes we'd already made came undone.

8.6Product page on the branch, before the redesign
6.4After the redesign
7Repeated styles behind around 19 contrast issues

The gap was in the process, not any one piece of work. At the Go/No Go meeting I suggested a simple check:

  1. Design stage

    Figma plugin check before handover.

  2. Before merge

    WAVE or Pope Tech before anything goes onto the branch or live.

  3. From tokens

    Contrast and type come from design system tokens, not one-off styles.

Design and dev are reviewing the product page contrast changes together, since they mean visible colour changes.

Outcome · Testing next

Testing with people, not just tools.

The brand doesn't run user testing yet, despite my pitches to bring in UserTesting. So for next week's manual testing sprint, I'm running a simple version in the office.

Office usability check

Planned
Who
Staff from across the business, outside the website team. Everyone takes the same test.
Focus
Readability and perceivability.
Set-up
Their usual device, then one task repeated at 200% browser zoom.
Tasks
  1. Find a product and read its details and price.
  2. Use the breadcrumbs to work out where you are.
  3. Add an item to the cart and read the delivery information.
  4. Repeat one task at 200% zoom.
Capture
Whether each task was completed, where people hesitated, and what they said.

Colleagues aren't a substitute for testing with disabled users, but it adds a human check alongside the automated tools.

Outcome · Learnings

What I'm taking from it.

Involve the builders early

Decisions made without the website team cost time and money.

Explain the why first

Framing it around legal risk and the brand's values got people listening.

A priority needs a process

Agreeing accessibility comes first wasn't enough without a shared check.

Tools only go so far

Scores made progress visible, but automated tools only catch part of WCAG.

Compromise, don't give up

Losing the 16px pitch didn't mean losing accessibility.
