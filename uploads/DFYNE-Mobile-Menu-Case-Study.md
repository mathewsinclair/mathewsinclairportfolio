# Fixing the thing that pissed me off

### Redesigning DFYNE's mobile navigation to make collections make sense — and diversify revenue while doing it

**Role:** Product / UX Designer (first project at DFYNE)
**Platform:** Mobile web (Shopify)
**Tools:** Microsoft Clarity, Figma, moderated guerrilla interviews
**Constraint:** Limited qualitative research budget. Quantitative signal from Microsoft Clarity only.
**Timeframe:** Design → launch → 7-day post-launch read

---

## The brief

"Fix something that pisses you off."

That was the whole assignment. No spec, no backlog ticket, no stakeholder wishlist — just permission to find a real problem and own it end to end. With almost no qualitative research budget and Microsoft Clarity as my only quantitative lens, I had to pick a problem I could actually prove and actually move.

So I picked the first thing that pissed me off as a new person on the site: **the mobile menu.**

---

## The problem

Opening the DFYNE mobile menu as a first-time visitor was disorienting. Two fundamentally different things were stacked on top of each other and styled identically:

- **Categories** — *what* the product is: Shorts, Sports Bras, T-Shirts & Tops, Hoodies, Joggers, Tracksuits.
- **Collections** — *who it's for and why it exists*: Impact, Vision, Defy, Dynamic, Signature, Revive, Origin, plus editor drops like The Hannah Pearson Edit.

Same font size. Same weight. Same spacing. Same layout. A flat wall of 40+ links per gender, repeated again for Mens, all inside one long scroll.

The audit made the core failure obvious: **there was no visual differentiation between "shop by category" and "shop by collection."** Categories and collections serve completely different mental models — one is a product type, the other is a brand story — but nothing on screen told you that. As a new user I had no idea what "Impact" or "Vision" meant, what made them different, or why I'd pick one over another.

Three problems, stacked:

1. **Cognitive overload.** A flat hierarchy of 40+ links with no chunking, no progressive disclosure, and no visual breaks. Users were confronted with everything at once.
2. **No collection identity.** Collections are DFYNE's editorial soul, but they read as just more line items. Nothing signalled *these all do something different.*
3. **Duplicated structure.** Mens repeated the exact same flat pattern in the same scroll, doubling the load and making it easy to lose track of whether you were even browsing Womens or Mens.

### The business problem hiding inside the UX problem

One collection — **Impact** — was doing the heavy lifting. It was the best seller and it was going viral on social, so it carried a hugely disproportionate share of revenue. Everything else sat in its shadow.

That's a fragile place for a brand to be. If new users can't understand collections, they default to the one collection they've already heard of on social. The menu wasn't just confusing — it was quietly concentrating revenue into a single line and leaving the rest of the range undiscovered.

**The opportunity:** help *new* users actually understand what collections are, while giving *Impact fans* a reason to explore the others — diversifying revenue and building real brand understanding at the same time.

---

## The research (working with what I had)

No budget for a formal study didn't mean no research. It meant faster, scrappier research.

**Quantitative — Microsoft Clarity.** I used Clarity to understand real behaviour: where people clicked, where they didn't, and how they moved through the menu. Two things stood out — reliance on search as an escape hatch from the menu, and a heavy concentration of engagement on Impact with a long tail of collections barely getting looked at.

**Qualitative — guerrilla moderated interviews.** I ran informal moderated sessions with friends, family, and new colleagues in the office. One question did most of the work:

> *"What is Defy? What is Vision?"*

Not one person answered *"it's the collection that…"* Nobody described them as collections at all. That was the validation I needed: the concept of a "collection" simply wasn't landing. If people can't tell you what a thing is, the navigation has already failed — and I was aiming at the right target.

---

## The insight

The words were fine. The **structure** was the problem.

Collections had no DNA. Each one — Impact, Vision, Defy, Revive — stands for something distinct, but the menu flattened them into identical text links, so users couldn't perceive difference, let alone act on it. The fix wasn't to write better copy. It was to give collections a visual system that screams: **these are not the same, and each one is worth exploring.**

---

## The solution

I took the long, undifferentiated list of collection names and turned them into **collection "pill" quick-buttons pinned to the top of the menu** — above the category list, in their own visual container.

Two moves did the heavy lifting:

**1. Pills create hierarchy.** Pulling collections up and out into horizontally-arranged quick-buttons instantly separates *"shop by collection"* (a brand story) from *"shop by category"* (a product type). One glance tells you these are two different ways to shop. The overload eases because collections are now a scannable, chunked row instead of buried in a 40-link scroll.

**2. Logos give each collection a DNA.** Each pill carries its own collection mark. That unique identity is the whole point — it signals, without a word of explanation, that **these all do something different.** A new user learns "collections are distinct worlds" just by looking. An Impact loyalist gets a visual hook — a differentiating feature — that makes Vision or Defy feel worth a tap instead of a scroll-past.

This is what solves both halves of the brief at once: new users finally get *what a collection is*, and Impact fans get a reason to explore beyond Impact.

---

## The results — 7-day post-launch (Microsoft Clarity)

I measured the launch against the 7 days prior, split by International and USA store.

### The menu is being used — search is being abandoned

Users shifted from searching to navigating through the menu. Search usage dropped **39–52% internationally** and **29–49% in the USA**. People stopped hunting for an escape hatch because the menu finally made sense — a clean adoption signal in both markets.

### Engagement surged on the hero collections

| Collection | International clicks | USA clicks |
|---|---|---|
| **Impact** | +93% (1,632 → 3,153) | +85% (2,294 → 3,933) |
| **Vision** | +311% (791 → 1,452) | +124% (805 → 1,699) |
| **Defy** | +18% | +32% |
| **Revive** | — | +134% (53 → 124) |

**Vision is the headline.** The collection I most wanted new users to discover jumped **+311% internationally** and **+124% in the USA** — exactly the diversification I designed for. Impact still grew strongly (proof the pills lifted the whole system, not just the underdogs), but the range around it finally started getting attention.

### Revenue followed engagement

Impact revenue rose **+8% internationally** and **+19% in the USA** ($807k → $884k) in the post-launch window — confirming a positive correlation between the new navigation and sales, not just clicks.

### The one-line story

> **Better navigation → collections understood → engagement spread beyond Impact → revenue up and diversifying.**

---

## What I'd do next (owning the trade-off)

A horizontal scroll on the pill row means the collections furthest right can hide off-screen. The post-launch data caught it: **Women's Signature and The Hannah Pearson Edit** sat in that hidden zone and showed flat-to-declining performance internationally (HP Edit down ~42%) while other collections climbed. The pattern is consistent enough to read as a placement problem, not a product problem.

If I carried this forward, I'd:

1. **Reduce the horizontal scroll** so every collection is visible without hunting — and deliberately protect the placement of Signature and HP Edit.
2. **Optimise pill ordering** — lead with proven engagement (Impact, Vision) but give underperforming collections better real estate rather than letting them fall off the edge.
3. **Track the full funnel** — menu click → collection page view → add to bag → purchase, reported separately for International and USA on a weekly cadence, so the primary KPI is revenue outcome, not just clicks.

---

## What this project taught me

- **A vague brief is a gift.** "Fix what pisses you off" let me find a problem with real business weight instead of a pre-chewed ticket.
- **Constraints sharpen research.** No budget forced me to pair Clarity behaviour data with one devastatingly simple interview question — and that combination was enough to validate direction with confidence.
- **A UX problem is often a business problem in disguise.** The menu wasn't just confusing; it was concentrating revenue into a single collection. Fixing comprehension diversified income.
- **Design the smallest thing that changes perception.** I didn't rewrite the menu. I gave collections a DNA — pills and logos — and that one shift moved clicks, revenue, and brand understanding together.
